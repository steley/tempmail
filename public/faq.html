Temp mail questions, answered directly

These are the twelve things people actually search for before using a disposable address — how long it lasts, what it refuses, and where it will not work. Where the answer is "no", it says no.

How long does a temporary email address last?

Mail is kept for about 30 minutes after the last message arrives, and the clock restarts with every new message. An address that is still receiving stays alive; one that goes quiet for half an hour is swept and its contents are gone.

The address itself is a different thing from the mailbox, and this is where most people are surprised. A temporary address is not registered to you and cannot be reserved. While your tab is open it is bound to your connection, and other people cannot take it. Once the tab closes, the name is released — after a short delay while the server notices the dropped connection, tens of seconds at most — and anyone can claim it, including someone else who wants the same custom name. What survives after that is not the name but the mail, for the remainder of its 30 minutes, reachable from the same browser or from a recovery link.

That is why the service has no "keep this address forever" option. Permanent addresses are what a normal mailbox provider sells; a 30-minute window is the entire product here, and it is what makes the service free, accountless, and unable to accumulate a profile of you.

What happens to my emails after 30 minutes?

They are permanently deleted. Not archived, not moved to a trash folder, not backed up anywhere. The mailbox is held in memory and a sweep timer removes it once the window closes. A recovery link stops working at the same moment, because the link points at that memory and nothing else.

Two practical consequences follow. First, copy anything you need — a confirmation code, a download link, a password-reset link — while the message is on screen. Once it is gone, no support request can bring it back; there is no store to search. Second, an inbox holds at most 20 messages: when the 21st arrives, the oldest one is dropped to keep memory bounded. A mailbox that gets flooded loses its oldest mail earlier than 30 minutes, so the message you are waiting for is safe, but the one you were about to read may not be.

If you need mail to persist, this is the wrong tool. Use it to receive a code and move on.

Can a temporary email receive attachments?

No. Attachments are stripped when the message is parsed, before it ever reaches the inbox. You get the text and HTML body, and nothing else. An invoice sent as a PDF arrives as the covering text with the file missing; an inline image referenced with cid: shows as a broken placeholder.

This is a deliberate limit, not a bug. Attachments are the most common delivery mechanism for malware, and storing multi-megabyte files for thousands of anonymous mailboxes would exhaust the server before anything else did. There is also no way to download one even if it were kept, since the interface only renders the body.

Two size limits sit behind that. A message larger than 5 MB is refused during the SMTP conversation, so the sender receives an error instead of silently believing it was delivered — useful when you are debugging why a service says "email sent" and nothing appears. And the parser forwards only headers, text and HTML, so an attachment cannot leak through a different field.

If a sender must send you a file, ask for a link instead: a share URL in the message body survives just fine.

Can I get a temp mail address with a password?

No, and there is no password field anywhere in the service. People search for "temp mail with password" because they want the one thing a disposable inbox normally cannot give: a way to come back to the same address later. So it is worth being precise about what exists instead.

A recovery link is the closest equivalent. Click it below the inbox and you get a private URL that reopens the same mailbox — with its recent mail — on any device, for about 30 minutes. Treat it exactly like a password: anyone who has the link has full read access to that inbox, and it cannot be revoked once copied.

What does not exist is protection on the address itself. A custom name is first come, first served and unowned: no password guards it, so if someone else knows the name they can open the same mailbox and read its live mail. That is the trade the service makes, and it means an address with a memorable name is not a private one.

For anything you would want to log back into, use a real mailbox provider. A disposable inbox is for the code you need once.

Is temp mail detectable by websites?

Yes, and you should assume it will be. Disposable domains are listed on public blocklists that many sign-up forms check automatically, sometimes before you submit and sometimes right after. When a site refuses, the rejection happens on their side: the message is never sent, so nothing reaches the inbox and no setting here can change it.

Because of that, this page will not tell you the service is "undetectable". Nothing on the client can hide the domain in the address you hand over. Any tool that promises otherwise is either describing a different mechanism — a real mailbox provider with throwaway aliases — or simply not telling the truth.

What you can do is choose the scenario. Low-stakes targets (newsletters, file downloads, trials, one-off forums, testing your own application) rarely block disposable domains and are exactly what this service is for. High-stakes targets (payment accounts, social accounts you want to keep, anything with recovery questions) block it more often and, even when they accept, tie you to an address that evaporates in half an hour.

Practical rule: if losing access would cost you something, do not use a disposable address for it.

Can I use temp mail for Facebook verification?

Sometimes, with caveats. Facebook is one of the stricter platforms: it frequently asks for a phone number instead of an email, and it may accept the address at sign-up only to reject the domain at the confirmation step. When it does work, the address stays usable for about 30 minutes — long enough to finish the flow.

The real problem is what happens afterwards. The account is bound to an address you will lose, so password recovery, suspicious-login checks and "confirm it's you" prompts all point at a mailbox that no longer exists. For a throwaway page used once, that is acceptable. For an account you intend to keep, it is a trap.

If you only need the code, keep the tab open until it arrives and copy it immediately. If the form insists on a phone number, this service cannot help — there is no SMS capability and adding one would defeat the point.

The same reasoning applies on other platforms; there are per-site notes in our Facebook, Instagram, Discord, Steam and Temu guides.

How do I open the same inbox on another device?

Two ways, depending on where you are starting from.

Same browser: just reopen the site. The address is saved locally, so it is restored automatically and the recent mail replays from the server's buffer. This also covers a reload, a crash, and a tab you closed by accident.

Different device or a cleared browser: click Recovery link below the inbox on the original device and open the copied URL on the other one within about 30 minutes. The link contains the token that identifies the mailbox, so it works from any browser.

A recovery link is a bearer credential: whoever opens it sees the mail. Send it to your own phone or laptop, never to a chat with someone else, and do not paste it anywhere public. Once the 30-minute window closes, the link is dead along with the mail, and no new one can be issued.

Why am I not receiving emails?

In order of how often each cause turns out to be the real one:

  • The sending site blocks disposable domains. This is by far the most common. Their form may even say "email sent" while nothing was transmitted, because the check happens after their own validation. If the waiting hints on the home page say the address looks blocked, this is why.
  • The address does not match exactly. Copy it with the copy button rather than retyping it; the alphabet deliberately omits i, l, o, 0 and 1, but a hand-typed name from a screenshot still goes wrong.
  • The message is over 5 MB and was refused at the SMTP stage. Senders usually report a bounce in that case; if they see nothing, this is not the cause.
  • It had an attachment and the body was empty. Attachments are stripped, and a message that was only an attachment arrives as nothing useful.
  • The page was closed. Live delivery needs the tab open. Reopen it in the same browser and the buffer replays.

What is not a cause: our rate limits. They cap how much one IP can send into the service per minute, not what a mailbox can receive, and legitimate sign-up mail never gets near them.

Can I choose my own temporary email address?

Yes. Click the edit button next to the address and type any name that is still free. The rules are deliberately narrow: 1 to 32 characters of a-z, 0-9, dot, underscore or hyphen, and it may not start or end with a dot or a hyphen. Uppercase is not accepted; names are lowercase only.

Names are allocated first come, first served, and nothing is reserved. There is no password and no ownership record, so a name that is free today may belong to someone else's tab tomorrow. That has one useful consequence — you can pick something readable, like a project name, instead of an eight-character scramble — and one dangerous one: a memorable name is guessable, and anyone who guesses it reads the mail.

For anything that matters, keep the random name. It is drawn from a 31-character alphabet with the confusable characters removed, which gives about 8.5×1011 combinations at the default length, and it is far harder to stumble into than myname.

Is a temporary inbox private?

No. A temporary inbox is public by construction: the address is the only credential, and anyone who knows it can open the same mailbox and watch mail arrive. This is a consequence of having no accounts, not an oversight that could be patched by adding a login — the moment there is a login, it stops being disposable.

What that rules out: confidential correspondence, banking and payment mail, anything containing identity documents, and password-reset flows for accounts you actually care about. What it is fine for: sign-up codes, download links, trials, newsletters you will never read, and testing mail delivery in your own application.

Two mechanical details worth knowing. Mail HTML is rendered inside a sandboxed frame, so a script in a message cannot reach the page or your saved address. But tracking pixels still load, which tells the sender that the message was opened — the same is true of nearly every webmail client. And inbound SMTP is unencrypted: this server does not offer STARTTLS, so a message can be read in transit by anyone positioned on the path. None of that matters for a verification code; all of it matters for anything you would not put on a postcard.

Can I send email from a temp mail address?

No. The service is receive-only. The SMTP endpoint accepts inbound mail for the domain and offers no authentication and no outbound relay, so there is nothing to log in to and no way to originate a message.

You cannot reply to a message, cannot forward one, and cannot use the address to contact support desks that require a reply from the same address. If a site's flow depends on replying to a confirmation mail, it will not complete here.

The upside of that restriction is that the domain cannot be used to send spam in your name, which is part of why it is not blocked more aggressively than it already is.

Do I need to install an app to use it on mobile?

No. TempMail.cc is a website and works in any current browser on Android and iOS. There is no app store listing, no download, and no account to create.

If you want an icon on your home screen, add the site to it: both Android Chrome and iOS Safari support that, and the installed version opens straight into a live inbox. It behaves like an app without being one — the mail still arrives over the network, so it needs a connection to work.

Desktop users get one extra feature: a QR code next to the address, which you can scan with a phone to open the same mailbox there. It is hidden on phones, where scanning your own screen would be pointless.
